What This Free Checker Does

This tool is a browser-based writing-originality checker. When you click "Check My Text," it scans your writing for dozens of commonly overused phrases — stock expressions like "in conclusion," "first and foremost," and "it is important to note" — and estimates how formulaic your text is. Everything runs locally in your browser: it works instantly, it's free, and your text is never uploaded.

Writing that leans on stock phrases tends to read as generic, and generic phrasing is also what accidental plagiarism, patch-writing, and template-driven drafts have in common. Spotting and rewriting those sections makes your writing more distinctive and less likely to overlap with existing content.

What Is a Plagiarism Checker?

The term covers two different kinds of tools. Database checkers compare your text against large indexes of web pages and papers to find matching sources — that's what schools and publishers typically use. Style-based checkers — like this one — analyze the text itself for signals such as overused phrasing. Database checkers find overlap with existing sources; style checkers help you write distinctively. Careful writers often benefit from both.

How to Interpret the Score

The score is an estimate of how much of your text relies on stock phrases, based on the ratio of flagged phrases to sentences. A high score means your phrasing is largely free of the clichés on our list; a low score means your text leans on formulaic language. It is a signal about writing style — not a verdict on whether your work is plagiarism-free. No automated tool can promise that, and we don't.

Common Causes of Repetitive or Low-Originality Writing

  • Template openings and closings — introductions built on "in today's society…" and conclusions built on "in conclusion…" are the fastest way to sound generic.
  • Patch-writing — swapping a few words in a source's sentence keeps its structure, which reads as derivative even when cited.
  • Transition filler — chains of "furthermore," "moreover," and "as a result of" pad word count without adding your own reasoning.
  • Summarizing without synthesizing — restating sources one by one instead of connecting them into your own argument.
  • Machine-translated or heavily AI-assisted drafts — generated boilerplate tends to converge on the same stock expressions.

How to Improve Originality Before Submitting Work

  • Write from an outline, not from a source — close the source before drafting so its sentence structure doesn't leak into yours.
  • Paraphrase ideas, then cite them anyway — changing the words doesn't remove the need for attribution.
  • Quote exact language — if the wording matters, put it in quotation marks with a citation instead of lightly editing it.
  • Replace stock transitions with transitions that say something: what follows, why it matters, or how it connects.
  • Read your draft aloud — clichés are easier to hear than to see.
  • Keep your drafts and notes — if a question about originality ever comes up, your working history is the best evidence the writing is yours.

Using This Tool for Academic Work — Read First

This tool can't predict that. Institutional checkers compare your text against databases of papers and web pages, which is a different kind of analysis. A high score here means your phrasing is distinctive — it is not a guarantee about source overlap.
Why did my quoted or cited text get flagged?
The checker flags common phrases wherever they appear, including inside quotations. Properly quoted and cited material isn't plagiarism — use the highlights to review your own prose, not your citations.
What actually counts as plagiarism?
Presenting someone else's words, ideas, structure, data, or media as your own — including reusing your own previously submitted work where the rules forbid it. Quotation marks, citations, and honest paraphrase with attribution are what keep borrowed material legitimate.
